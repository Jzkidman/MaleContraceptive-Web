A new hormone-free male contraceptive pill, YCT-529, 
has passed early human safety trials with no reported 
side effects and works by blocking a vitamin A receptor 
essential for sperm production. 
The pill has been making headlines and has sparked controversial topics about the pill itself as previous trials had been discontinued due to “side effect being adverse”, many 
of which are common for the female contraceptive pill.
This infographic collates the information gathered from 
an even split of male and female participants.

Side effects may include severe 
trust deficits, with average trust 
levels plateauing at only 23.15% 
for male contraceptive responsibility 
(focus on remembering daily consumption).
This can additionally be seen with 
trust as a dominant pattern, with 73% 
showing minimal confidence (≤30%) 
in male contraceptive compliance, suggesting 
deep-rooted skepticism about male reliability 
in reproductive health.
Memory-based skepticism drives much of the resistance, as 76% of female participants express concerns about men forgetting, being unreliable, 
or not taking daily medication seriously (≤30% trust), highlighting perceived differences in health responsibility between genders.

Side effects may include widespread adoption pessimism, 
with predicted male uptake averaging just 16.38% 
if available immediately.
When flipped around, when male participants asked if they would go onto a theortical male pill that had the same side effects as the female pill, 54% said no.
This culminated in participants averaging that approximately 27.29% would adopt the pill, with over 50% of participants giving pessimistic predictions (≤20% adoption) and only 
7.1% were optimistic of the release (>50% adoption).

Side effects may include massive responsibility imbalance, with women currently handling 75.6% of contraceptive burden according to respondent estimates.
Communication burden concentration adds another layer to female responsibility, with women initiating 71.04% of sexual health conversations in relationships, suggesting men typically avoid discussing contraceptive topics.
This culminates in the fact that 57.1% of participants believe 
that in 25 years, it will continue to be mostly women who will 
be the primary contraceptive handler.
